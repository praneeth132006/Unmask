What happened

An email with the subject “[Binance] Withdraw Successful - 2023-07-30 51:51:51(UTC)” arrived from “Binance” <noreply-supportbinancewallet[.]irs[@]auswestbc[.]com[.]au>. It was examined entirely on the analyst's computer: its sender details, its links, its attachments and the way it was delivered.

It looks like impersonation (high confidence): It pretends to be a company or person you trust, from an address that isn't theirs. It looks like password phishing (low confidence): It wants you to sign in on a fake page or type your password.

The main reasons

  • It pretends to be Binance, but Binance didn’t send it.

Why it matters to the organisation

RiskWhat could happenLevel
Trust and brand abuseStaff, customers or partners are tricked by a convincing fake of a person or company they trust.Medium
Stolen passwordsSomeone who enters a password on the fake page hands attackers the account. From there they can read mail and files, send more phishing from a trusted address and reach other systems.High
PrivacyThe email contains 1 hidden tracking pixel. When the email is opened, the sender learns it was read and roughly where.Low

What to do now

Everyone

  • Do not click links, open attachments, scan QR codes or reply to this email.
  • Do not forward it to colleagues. Report it using the steps below, then delete it.
  • If you are unsure whether it is genuine, contact the supposed sender using a phone number or website you already know, never the details in the email.

If someone already clicked, opened a file, replied or typed a password

  • Tell the IT or security team immediately. Speed matters more than embarrassment.
  • Change the password for that account, and for any other account that uses the same password, from a different device.
  • If a file was opened, disconnect the computer from the network and wait for IT before using it again.
  • If money was sent or bank details were shared, call the bank at once and ask for the payment to be recalled.

For the IT and security team

  • Block the sender, the domains and the links listed in the technical part, and search every mailbox for the same email.
  • Remove copies from all mailboxes that received it.
  • For anyone who interacted with it: reset passwords, sign them out everywhere, review app approvals and check for new mailbox rules or forwarding.

Who to tell

  • Your internal IT or security team (first, always).
  • Anti-Phishing Working Group (APWG): reportphishing@apwg.org
  • Netcraft: scam@netcraft.com, for takedown of the fake sites
  • Google Safe Browsing: https://safebrowsing.google.com/safebrowsing/report_phish/
  • United States: https://reportfraud.ftc.gov and https://www.ic3.gov
  • United Kingdom: report@phishing.gov.uk (NCSC)
  • India: https://cybercrime.gov.in and incident@cert-in.org.in (CERT-In)
  • Elsewhere: your national CERT or cybercrime reporting centre. Serious losses should also be reported to the police and the bank.

Containment and remediation checklist

  • Preserve the original message (.eml) and this report before deleting anything.
  • Identify every recipient (mail logs) and who clicked or opened attachments (proxy, DNS and endpoint logs).
  • Purge the message from all mailboxes and quarantine matching ones.
  • Block the sender, domains, URLs and hashes listed above.
  • For affected users: reset passwords, revoke sessions and refresh tokens, review OAuth app consents, MFA methods, mailbox rules, forwarding and delegates.
  • Report externally (APWG, Netcraft, national CERT) and notify any impersonated brand.
  • Run a short awareness note to staff with the key signs from this email.
  • Record the outcome and close the case.

Method, limits and references

What was checked

  • Sender identity, header consistency and authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC, ARC), including a local DKIM body-hash re-check.
  • Delivery path and timing from Received headers.
  • Links: real destinations, lookalike domains, redirects, hosting services, QR codes.
  • Body: hidden text, obfuscation, urgency and payment language, tracking pixels.
  • Attachments: file type by content, structure of PDFs, Office files, RTF and archives, macros, embedded files, links and indicators, hashes.

Not checked

  • A DKIM signature was not verified with the signer’s key (turn on “Check certificate history and mail records” in Settings).
  • 10 links were checked by address only. What the page does is unknown until you run a sandbox scan from the Links tab.
  • Remote images were not loaded, so text inside them wasn’t read.
