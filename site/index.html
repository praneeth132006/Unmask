See what a suspicious email is really hiding.

Unmask is a free browser extension for Gmail and Outlook. It stops you before you click a phishing link, warns you right inside your inbox, and explains in plain language who really sent the email, where its links and QR codes go, and what its attachments hide.

It stops the click

The email shows https://www.paypal.com/signin. The link really goes to paypa1-secure.top. Unmask catches it before the page opens and tells you why.

Check an email before you open it

Opening an email loads its hidden tracking pixels and tells the sender you read it. Click 👁 Peek in the inbox instead: Unmask checks the email and shows a text-only preview, so nothing in it ever loads.

Warnings right inside your inbox

No need to open anything. Unmask checks each email as you open it and marks risky senders in the list.

Install in two minutes

Chrome, Edge and Brave: install from the Chrome Web Store in one click, or get the Firefox add-on. Chrome may say the extension is "not trusted by Enhanced Safe Browsing" while it is new; click Continue to install. Prefer to load it yourself? Follow the steps below. No programming needed.

Download

From the latest release, download the zip for your browser under Assets (unmask-chrome, unmask-edge or unmask-firefox) and unzip it. Keep the folder somewhere permanent.

Load it

Open chrome://extensions, switch on Developer mode, click Load unpacked and choose the unzipped folder (the one containing manifest.json). Firefox: about:debugging → Load Temporary Add-on.

Check an email

Reload Gmail or Outlook and open any email. Risky ones get a warning straight away; click the Unmask icon for the full report.

Everything a phishing email tries to hide

Every check runs inside your browser. The few online lookups send only a domain name or a file fingerprint, and each can be turned off.

Sender

Who really sent it

SPF, DKIM and DMARC, display names hiding another address, and look-alike domains like paypa1.com for 50+ brands, using Unicode's confusables list.

0.4

Header forensics

DKIM signatures verified by Unmask itself, emails changed after signing, fake "passed" stamps, replayed genuine emails, and the delivery path read the way an analyst does.

0.4

Attack techniques

Callback scams, real PayPal and DocuSign emails carrying a scammer's text, device-code and consent lures, ClickFix, locked ZIPs sent with their password.

0.4

Encoding tricks

Words spelt in HTML character codes, disguised link addresses, invisible characters splitting brand names, and letters from other alphabets.

0.4

Analyst write-up

What kind of attack it is, what couldn't be checked, a SOC-style case report and defanged indicators, one click each.

Links

Where links really go

Text that shows one site but links to another, redirect wrappers removed, and one-click redirect tracing.

Hover

Point at the problem

Hover a red flag and Unmask outlines the exact link, sender, attachment or phrase in the Gmail email.

Files

Risky attachments

Executables, fake PDFs (Invoice.pdf.html), macro documents, encrypted ZIPs, plus a VirusTotal check.

Body

Hidden text, safely shown

Invisible text used to fool spam filters, forms asking for passwords, and pressure phrases, in a safe view with scripts off.

Privacy

Trackers

Tracking pixels and click-tracked links from about 40 services, with images blocked so senders can't tell you opened it.

Private by design

Stays on your device

The email is analyzed in your browser and never sent to an Unmask server. There are no accounts and no analytics.

Online checks are opt-in

VirusTotal and urlscan.io run only when you click them, and get a link or a file's fingerprint, not the email. Sandbox scans are always private: only your key can see them.

Free, and never billed

Free keys can't be charged; going over a limit just pauses that check. Limits and costs.

Unmask never says an email is "safe". A clean result means the checks found nothing, not that nothing is there. Stay careful with links and attachments you didn't expect.
